Project 02

Conspiracy Theory Rhetorical Analysis.

A rhetorical analysis of conspiracy writing about MKUltra's safehouses in San Francisco. Looks at how the writing anchors on declassified Church Committee material and then extends those documents into claims they don't actually support. Submitted as a written essay and a short video.

Video not loading? Open the MP4 directly.

The argument

Conspiracy writing about MKUltra in the Bay Area tends to follow a pattern I called "anchor-then-drift." It anchors on documented stuff — the CIA's Technical Services Staff, Sidney Gottlieb's chemistry program, Operation Midnight Climax's safehouses on Telegraph Hill, the Church Committee's 1975 findings — and then extends that documented evidence into claims the documents don't support: that the safehouses caused Haight-Ashbury counterculture, that Ken Kesey's Acid Tests were a CIA operation, that the 1973 destruction of MKUltra files is evidence of guilt.

The argument is that the persuasive force comes from the citation structure itself, not from any one false claim. The redactions are real; the documents are real; but the conspiracy genre uses the redactions as positive evidence for the parts that aren't documented.

Rhetorical concepts the essay uses

  • Synecdoche — the safehouse standing in for the program.
  • Enthymeme — the unstated premise that redaction implies suppression.
  • Kairos — why post-Snowden audiences read MKUltra claims differently than 1970s ones did.
  • Doxa — assumptions about what the security state will do.
  • Anchor-then-drift — the structural move I'm naming.

Sources

  • U.S. Senate, Final Report of the Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations (Church Committee), 1976.
  • Stephen Kinzer, Poisoner in Chief.
  • Tom O'Neill, Chaos: Charles Manson, the CIA, and the Secret History of the Sixties.
  • Martin A. Lee & Bruce Shlain, Acid Dreams.
  • John Marks, The Search for the Manchurian Candidate.
  • Timothy Melley on the "covert sphere."

Process

The first draft (Week 10) made the same slippery-slope move I was supposed to be critiquing. The instructor's feedback flagged it, and most of the revision was about pulling that back — distinguishing what's documented from what's inferred, and being explicit about which is which.

Your most precise and sophisticated use of that fallacy across all 35 documents is identifying how Soviet threat framing redirected scrutiny away from CIA misconduct.

What I would change

Too many sources. The essay tries to give equal time to six different bodies of work and ends up shallower than it needs to be. Next time I'd keep the Church Committee record and Kinzer as the spine and fold the rest into a shorter discussion of the genre.
