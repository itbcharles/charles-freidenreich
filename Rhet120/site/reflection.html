component 02 · reflection essay (~1,540 words, five goals)

RHET 120 is the general-education writing requirement at USF, and I took it as one of the standard first-year sequence. The course covered rhetorical analysis, multimodal composition, and a long research project, and the final assignment asks for a reflection on five goals from the semester. The five I picked are three from the course outcomes (rhetorical knowledge, critical reading, composing processes) and two personal ones I set in the first week (write in a medium I had not used before, and learn to be more honest about what I am not sure of in my own writing). The essay below moves through them in order. I have tried to be specific about what changed and not overstate it.

(1) rhetorical knowledge i can actually use. The first weeks of the class covered the basic terms — ethos, pathos, logos, and the less-familiar kairos, mythos, and doxa. Learning the words was easy. The harder part was learning to use them on real writing. My first MKUltra paragraph, drafted in Week 10, was basically a slippery-slope argument that the CIA's chemistry program caused Haight-Ashbury counterculture. By the final draft I had a more limited claim: that conspiracy literature uses one declassified site, the Telegraph Hill safehouses, as a synecdoche standing in for an unproven larger pattern. The point is not that I had a big insight; it is that having names for these moves makes them easier to catch in my own first drafts. I also learned, more or less, that an appeal that works on one audience in one moment will not necessarily work on another. In my Week 11 reflection I noted that MKUltra claims land harder after Snowden than they did in the 1970s, because what readers expect from a security agency has shifted. That is a useful thing to keep in mind, but I would not say it changed how I think — it gave me a name for something I sort of already noticed.

(2) read critically, including my own drafts. The thing I had to read most carefully this semester was my own first draft of the CT essay. Reading it back, I noticed I was citing the existence of redactions in the Church Committee documents as if those redactions covered the specific claims I was making. They don't. The evidence was real, the conclusion was speculative, and what was holding them together was just sentence order. Catching that is the kind of critical reading the course was actually trying to teach. The smaller-scale version was the Trump-Iran logos exercise. The quoted argument sounds logical but runs on a buried premise about military presence as deterrence. Naming the premise was the whole exercise. I still apply it when I read political speech or ad copy, but I am not going to pretend it changed me — it is a habit, not a transformation. The longer pieces we read in the course (Kinzer, O'Neill, Melley) helped with this in a different way. Reading 200 pages of trade nonfiction about MKUltra, and then reading a 10-page Melley essay about the rhetoric of secrecy, makes you notice how differently the two kinds of writing handle the same evidence. The trade book wants you to feel something; the academic essay wants you to notice how the trade book is making you feel something. Both moves are useful. Both are easier to spot once you have seen the same material handled both ways.

(3) treat composing as a process, not a single act. Before this class I usually drafted once and revised lightly. The DLN broke that habit, mostly because it had to. Once I had a script I liked, recording it out loud showed me pacing problems and clunky sentences that silent reading didn't. I had to keep revising even though the writing felt finished. Peer review on Petra's DLN draft and the memoir-revision workshop did smaller versions of the same thing. The memoir worksheet in particular makes the changes concrete — same paragraph, four different revision passes, four pretty different results. I also did a small amount of planning before drafting on the CT project, in the form of the mind map comparing newsletter and podcast formats. That was the only time all semester I planned a piece before starting, and it was probably worth doing. None of this is groundbreaking — most writing classes teach revision — but I had not previously made it part of my process. The peer-review part is worth noting separately. Most of the peer reviews I gave this semester were of the "looks good, maybe tighten the intro" variety, which is more or less useless. The one I am most embarrassed about is from Week 3, and the one I am least embarrassed about is the response to Petra's draft, which is in the artifact collection. The difference is just specificity. Saying "your second scene works better than your first because the second one shows action" is more useful than "I liked it." This is, again, not a big revelation — but I had been getting away with vague peer feedback for a long time.

(4) try a medium i had not used. My first personal goal was to actually work in a medium I usually avoid. I write essays and code; I had never edited a video for a class, and I had never written about conspiracy literature with the intent to take it seriously as a genre. The DLN was the first; the CT project was the second. The video taught me something concrete about pacing — sentences that read fine in the script were unworkable on tape — but I do not want to oversell it. It is also possible I would have figured that out the first time I had to record any video for any reason. Still, doing it for a graded assignment made me actually do it, which is more than I can say for most modalities I "intend to try." The CT project counts under this goal too, in a smaller way. I had read conspiracy theories before, mostly online and mostly to argue with them. Writing 5,000 words about how the genre's writing works, instead of about whether the claims are true, was a different exercise. It does not mean I now think differently about the underlying claims (I do not), but I do read this kind of writing more carefully now, which seems like the actual point.

(5) be more honest about uncertainty. My other personal goal was harder to define at the start. My default writing voice is confident, and I tend to overclaim in first drafts. The CT project was the first thing I wrote where I tried, on purpose, to mark which claims were speculative and which were documented. The instructor's feedback flagged that distinction as the thing that made the analysis work, which I think is the best evidence I have that I did it on purpose. The DLN required something similar — I had to avoid making my third-grade self sound more aware than he was. The earlier "Discovering my own digital literacy" essay (Week 2) does overclaim; the DLN narration walks it back. I would say I made progress here, but I would not say I have it under control. Confident prose is faster, and I default back to it whenever I am not paying attention.

Closing it out: three takeaways. First, kairos matters more than I gave it credit for at the start — the same words land differently depending on when they are read. Second, switching modes (page to video, essay to short video version of the same essay) makes you notice things in your writing you would not have noticed in just one medium. Third, sources are an audience contract. Citing a lot of real material does not automatically make an argument true; the join between citation and conclusion is its own thing, and a careful reader will check it. None of this is going to win anyone a Pulitzer. It is what I learned in a one-semester GE writing class. The portfolio collects the work that produced these takeaways, including the parts where I got it wrong on the first try. The thing I am still working on is the calibration goal — the temptation to overstate is constant, and the artifacts here are mostly the moments I caught myself and stopped. If next semester I had to do this same exercise over again, I would probably set a smaller and more specific personal goal at the start — something like "use peer review for actual feedback, not validation," which is the kind of habit I can measure in any class, not just a writing one. That is a more useful framing than "become a better writer," which I picked at the start of the semester and which is too vague to be honest about whether I hit.
